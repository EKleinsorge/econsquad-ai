The Monday AI for ED Drop — Issue #14: Site Scorecards That Make Decisions Easier

August 11, 20264 min read

Site selection decisions are rarely made on a single factor. They involve dozens of variables weighted differently by each company, evaluated across multiple locations in a compressed timeline. A site scorecard—when built correctly—provides the structure that turns a complex multi-variable decision into a clear recommendation. Here is how to build better ones.

This Week’s Tips

Tip 1

Build Industry-Specific Scorecard Templates

A scorecard built for a distribution center should look very different from one built for a food processing facility or a professional services office. The variables that matter most differ by industry, and a generic scorecard wastes everyone’s time. Prompt your AI tool: “Build a site scorecard template for evaluating locations for a [light industrial / food processing / advanced manufacturing / distribution] facility. Include the top 12 to 15 evaluation criteria most important for this sector, organized into categories. For each criterion, suggest a scoring scale and explain why it matters to this type of project. Then identify the top three criteria that typically carry the most weight in final decisions.” Build one template per major sector you target, then adapt for each deal.

Tip 2

Use Weighted Scoring to Reduce Subjectivity

A scorecard with equal weights for all criteria will produce inconsistent decisions because evaluators will unconsciously weight the criteria they personally care most about. Before every major evaluation, prompt: “For this type of project, here are the site selection criteria I plan to use. Help me assign weights to each criterion based on what matters most to a typical company in this sector and for this project type. Weights should sum to 100. Explain the reasoning for any weight above 10% or below 5%.” Then use those weights consistently across sites. Weighted scores make your comparison defensible to elected officials, boards, and the company itself.

Tip 3

Present Scorecards as a Decision Story, Not a Spreadsheet

Even a well-built scorecard can fail to drive a decision if the presentation buries the conclusion in numbers. After completing your evaluation, prompt: “Here are the scores for three candidate sites. Based on the scores and weights, write a two-paragraph executive summary that explains which site scores highest, what the key differentiating factors are, and what the most significant tradeoffs are between the top two options. Write it so that a decision-maker who has not reviewed the full scorecard could make a confident recommendation from this summary alone.” The executive summary turns a technical analysis into a decision support tool.
