The Monday AI for ED Drop — Issue #16: Board Reports That Build Confidence and Drive Decisions

August 25, 20264 min read

The board report is one of the most important and most underinvested communications in economic development. Your board controls your budget, your mandate, and your relationship with elected officials. If they do not understand what you are doing and why it matters, every ask you make is harder. Here is how to use AI to build board reports that actually work.

This Week’s Tips

Tip 1

Structure Reports Around Decisions, Not Activities

The most common board report mistake is organizing by activity: here is what we did this quarter. A board that only hears activity updates starts to wonder what the strategy is. Structure your report around decisions instead: here is the decision we are asking you to make, here is the information you need to make it, and here is our recommendation. For the sections that are genuinely informational, prompt your AI tool: “I need to summarize our Q3 activity for our board. Here are the key items. For each one, help me frame it as a strategic progress update: what was the goal, what did we do, what is the current status, and what does the board need to know or decide as a result?”

Tip 2

Anticipate the Questions Before the Meeting

Every board meeting has predictable questions: what is the ROI on this program, why did we lose that deal, how does our performance compare to peers. Anticipating those questions and answering them in the report saves meeting time and signals confidence. Before finalizing your board report, prompt: “Here is our draft board report. What are the five most likely questions a skeptical board member would ask after reading this? For each question, draft a concise answer that acknowledges the concern and provides supporting data or context.” Add the answers as footnotes, appendices, or prepare them as talking point cards. Either way, you walk in prepared.

Tip 3

Write the Executive Summary Last, Lead With It

Most people write their executive summary first as an outline, then fill in the detail. The result is a summary that does not actually summarize what is most important. Write your full report first, then prompt: “Here is our full board report. Write a half-page executive summary that: leads with the most important thing the board needs to know from this quarter, covers the two or three decisions they need to make, and closes with the forward-looking priority for next quarter. Write it so a board member who only reads this page walks away with the right picture.” That summary goes at the front. The detail goes behind it.
