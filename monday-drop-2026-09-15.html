The Monday AI for ED Drop — Issue #19: Cover Letters and Proposals That Open Doors

September 15, 20264 min read

In economic development, cover letters and proposals do two very different jobs. The cover letter’s job is to make the reviewer want to read the proposal. The proposal’s job is to make the case so compellingly that saying yes feels obvious. Most organizations get one of these right. Here is how AI helps you get both right, consistently.

This Week’s Tips

Tip 1

Write the Cover Letter After You Know Your Strongest Points

Most cover letters are written at the start of the process, before the writer knows what is most compelling about the proposal. Write your proposal first, then prompt: “Here is my proposal. What are the two or three points in this document that are most likely to be differentiating to a reviewer who has read many similar submissions? Write a cover letter that leads with those points, sets context for the full proposal, and closes with a clear invitation to continue the conversation. Do not summarize everything—make them want to read more.” A cover letter written after the proposal is almost always better than one written before it.

Tip 2

Match Your Proposal’s Language to the Reviewer’s Language

Grant reviewers, site consultants, and RFP evaluators are trained to look for specific language, frameworks, and evidence types. A proposal that uses your organization’s internal vocabulary may not resonate with reviewers who use different terminology for the same concepts. Before finalizing your proposal, prompt: “Here is my proposal and here is the RFP or NOFO it is responding to. Identify any places where my language may not match the reviewers’ language or stated priorities. Suggest specific replacements that use the terminology from the RFP while preserving my original meaning.” Language alignment is a small change that can meaningfully affect how well your proposal scores.

Tip 3

Test Your Proposal Against the Evaluation Criteria

Most proposals are evaluated against explicit criteria with explicit point values. Treat those criteria as a checklist, not background reading. Before submitting, prompt: “Here are the evaluation criteria for this proposal, with their point weights. Review my proposal against each criterion and score it 1 to 5 on how well it currently addresses each one. For any criterion below a 4, suggest specific content additions or revisions that would strengthen the response.” This pre-submission review catches gaps that are invisible when you are deep in the writing but obvious once you step back and score objectively.
