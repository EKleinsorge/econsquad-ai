The Monday AI for ED Drop — Issue #12: Annual Reports That People Actually Read

July 28, 20264 min read

The annual report is one of the most important communications an ED organization produces and one of the least read. A document that takes weeks to compile lands in inboxes and gets skimmed for three minutes before being filed. Here is how to change that by using AI to build an annual report that people actually want to read.

This Week’s Tips

Tip 1

Lead With the Impact Number, Not the Activity Number

Most annual reports organize around what the organization did: meetings held, inquiries fielded, applications submitted. What stakeholders actually care about is what changed because of that work. Before writing your report, prompt: “Here is a list of our organization’s activities from this year. For each one, help me reframe it as an impact statement: not what we did, but what changed as a result. If the impact is indirect, help me estimate the connection.” Then prompt: “What is the single most compelling statistic from this list that tells the story of a year that mattered?” Lead with that number. Everything else is context.

Tip 2

Build Sections Around Stakeholder Questions

Different readers want different things from your annual report. Your board wants ROI on investment. Elected officials want constituent impact. Site selectors want signals of competitiveness. Local media wants a story. Structure your report to answer the question each audience is actually asking, not just to document your activities. Prompt: “Here are the three to four main audiences for our annual report. For each audience, write a one-paragraph executive summary of our year that answers the question they are most likely asking when they open this document.” Use each summary as the section header for that audience’s portion of the report.

Tip 3

Turn Data Into Narrative Before It Becomes Bullet Points

Most annual reports kill momentum with tables and bullet lists that make readers feel like they are reviewing a spreadsheet, not reading a story about their community. The antidote is narrative. When you have a data section to write, prompt: “Here are the key metrics from our [workforce / real estate / business climate / investment] data this year. Write a three-paragraph narrative that turns these numbers into a story. What does the trend mean? What should a reader feel about the direction we are headed? What does this data say about the community’s competitive position?” Numbers without narrative are just trivia. Narrative with data is evidence.
